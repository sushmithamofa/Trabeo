**Yes, it makes sense.** I'd agree with about 90% of it. I didn't open the code to check the cited line numbers, so this is based on what I know of the build.

## Where it's right

**The five issues are real, and four of them break your own rule.** You asked for no built-in standards: every value should come from the drawings. Yet these are all hardcoded guesses that still get built:
- 450 mm beam depth
- 150 mm slab thickness
- footings 1,500 mm below the plan level
- 3,000 mm per storey

The review screen flags conflicts, but the export still builds the guessed geometry anyway. Because the result looks finished, that's the most dangerous gap for a paid product. Fixing this comes first.

**Multiple plans in model space and units** are also correct. Consultants often put several floors side by side in one DWG. Units are the first thing that will break once you go international.

**The point about the "blind" test is fair.** A synthetic drawing set catches regressions but doesn't show the app works on drawings it has never seen. You need 3–5 real projects from consultants who weren't used during development before you claim accuracy.

**Limiting the first release to a stated scope is the right commercial call.** For example: reinforced-concrete beams, columns, slabs, walls and isolated footings. The suggested promise ("supported drawings → editable Revit elements, with evidence and review") is honest and sellable.

## Where I'd adjust it

1. **Revision-aware updates are a second-release feature.** They're valuable, but hard: you have to match elements across revisions and protect the user's manual edits. A pilot customer can live with "rebuild from the new drawings" at first.
2. **ODA Drawings SDK:** its licence has a yearly cost. Check whether the current conversion path is really failing before taking that on. It matters most once you have an installer.
3. **AI interpretation layer:** they're right to put it after the validation fixes. It should only suggest readings for the user to review. It should never place geometry directly.
4. **Installer, licensing and updates** matter for the commercial release, not the pilot. A supervised pilot can run with you doing the setup.

## Recommended order

1. **Block unresolved data before building.** Remove every hardcoded default. If a value is missing, the element is marked "needs a decision" and isn't built.
2. **Founding levels per element and confirmed floor elevations.** Both should be read from the drawings or entered by the user, never assumed.
3. **Units:** detect and convert them, or stop if the units are unclear.
4. **Multiple plans in model space:** split them by plan title and geometry clusters.
5. Test on real, unseen consultant projects and measure positions, dimensions and elevations, not just element counts.
6. Then run a paid pilot, and after that build the installer, revision updates and the AI layer.

Bottom line: keep the architecture. The review describes the gap between a good prototype and something you can charge for, and steps 1–4 close the most serious part of that gap.

## What was fixed

1. **Conflicts no longer reach Revit.** An element whose size is in conflict, guessed or missing is now left out. It is listed under "Needs a decision" until you settle it in the review. 4. **Several floors side by side in one drawing are read one at a time.** Before, only the first floor was read. A drawing with one floor is read exactly as before.
5. **Units.** Metres and centimetres are converted to millimetres. Feet-and-inch drawings, and drawings whose units can't be told, are stopped with a plain message, and you can state the units yourself.
